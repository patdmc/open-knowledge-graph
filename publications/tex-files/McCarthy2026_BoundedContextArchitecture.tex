\begin{abstract}
Any system that accumulates knowledge under bounded active context
faces a fundamental tradeoff between consistency and availability.
We show that this single constraint---bounded context---predicts
four architectural consequences that independently emerge across
software, biological, and organizational systems.

First, a directed graph with typed edges is the cost-optimal
representation: factoring shared structure preserves information
while reducing context load, and sub-linear dependency queries
require stored adjacency. Second, as action repertoire grows,
control architectures converge toward escalation: lower levels
resolve locally, signaling upward only on failure. Third, shared
propositions precipitate from the action space into a separated
knowledge structure---normalization in Codd's sense---eliminating
the update anomalies that manifest as catastrophic forgetting in
architectures without partition boundaries. Fourth, agency
($\gamma > 0$) and learning efficiency ($\eta_M > 0$) are the
minimal structural invariants of persistence under positive novelty
rate; their product $I(E) = \gamma \cdot \eta_M$ is a natural
measure of intelligence as rate of adaptation to novelty.

These results are not independent insights but consequences of one
constraint. Empirical confirmation comes from cancer genomics:
grouping mutations by coupling-channel boundaries (the graph's
partition structure) predicts overall survival with $p < 10^{-14}$
across 71,983 patients, while total mutation count does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Any system that persists in a physical world is caught between two
forces. From above: entropy is positive, the world produces novel
states, and an entity that does not grow its knowledge $K$ eventually
encounters a state it cannot handle. Growth is not optional; it is
forced by the novelty rate that entropy guarantees. From below:
active context $C_n$ is bounded, and $|K|$ eventually exceeds $C_n$.
The system must structure what it knows or drown in its own
knowledge. These two forces---growth pressure from entropy, capacity
constraint from bounded context---define the problem. Everything in
this paper follows from their intersection.

The constraint admits exactly two responses: minimize the need for
context or maximize context capacity. Strategy~1 keeps $|K|$ small
(fungible units, no hierarchy, partition tolerance through
redundancy). Bacteria, BitTorrent swarms, and transformer attention
layers all follow this pattern: flat, replaceable, and robust by sheer
count. Strategy~2 structures $K$ into addressable hierarchy (typed
edges, scoped domains, sub-linear retrieval). At small scale,
Strategy~1 does not merely suffice---it \emph{outperforms}. When
$|K| \ll C_n$, exhaustive search is faster than indexed retrieval
because the constant dominates the exponent. The dangerous property of
the inferior scaling class is not that it fails but that it succeeds,
conspicuously, until a crossover it cannot see.

\emph{Physarum polycephalum} (slime mold) provides a striking
existence proof that the crossover is real. Under abundant resources,
\emph{Physarum} persists as individual fungible cells (Strategy~1).
Under scarcity, the cells aggregate into a tubular network that solves
shortest-path problems through physical flow dynamics alone
\cite{Nakagaki2000}: parallel exploration followed by topological
optimization. No neuron, no central controller, no designer. The same
cells, the same genome, switching from swarm to graph when the
pressure crosses the threshold. Graph structure emerges from the
constraint, not from design. Large language models are undergoing the
same transition now: retrieval-augmented generation, tool use, and
agent architectures are graph structure aggregating around a flat
substrate under scaling pressure.

Software exhibits this universally. Every system contains directed
adjacency with typed edges across multiple simultaneous substrates:
call graphs, import graphs, type graphs, dataflow graphs, service
graphs, schema graphs, build graphs, and deployment graphs. The graph
property is not an architectural choice but intrinsic to what software
is. The same graphs appear in biological systems: gene regulatory
networks, protein interaction networks, metabolic pathways, neural
connectivity, and the coupling channels that coordinate multicellular
behavior.

This paper shows that one constraint---bounded active context---predicts
four architectural consequences that independently emerge across these
domains:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Graph structure} is cost-optimal for managing consistency
   and availability under bounded context
   (Section~\ref{sec:graph}).
\item \textbf{Escalation} is the cost-optimal control architecture:
   handle locally, escalate on failure
   (Section~\ref{sec:escalation}).
\item \textbf{Normalization} of shared propositions into a separated
   knowledge structure is the cost-optimal consistency mechanism
   (Section~\ref{sec:normalization}).
\item \textbf{Agency and learning efficiency} are the minimal
   structural invariants of persistence under positive novelty rate
   (Section~\ref{sec:intelligence}).
\end{enumerate}

These are not independent insights but consequences of one constraint.
Cancer, viewed from this framing, is the defection of cells from the
graph architecture back to swarm: severing the coupling channels that
carry the topology \cite{McCarthy2026e}. In cancer genomics, grouping
mutations by coupling-channel boundaries---the graph's partition
structure---predicts overall survival with $p < 10^{-14}$ across
71,983 patients, while total mutation count does not
(Section~\ref{sec:empirical}).

Throughout this paper, ``cost-optimal'' means selectively favored under
competition: entities paying higher costs for the same operations lose
resources to competitors paying less. Under sustained competition,
cost-optimal structure is selected for and alternatives are eliminated.
This is selective necessity, not logical impossibility of alternatives.
